# Nigerian Draw Classification — Is Atlas's Prize-Competition Model Defensible?

> **This is not legal advice.** It is desk research by an AI assistant, assembled from
> publicly available sources on 2026-11-23, to help decide *whether and at what scope to
> engage Nigerian counsel*. Nothing here should be relied on for a launch decision. Several
> of the most commercially significant figures below are reported by secondary sources only
> and are explicitly flagged as unverified. Primary statutes were read where obtainable;
> the key one (National Lottery Act 2005) has been judicially nullified, which changes the
> entire frame of the question Atlas originally scoped in June 2026.

---

## Executive summary

**Atlas's central legal premise is out of date, and the model it rests on does not work in
Nigeria.** On 22 November 2024 the Supreme Court of Nigeria, in *AG Lagos State & Ors v AG
Federation* (SC/1/2008), held that lottery and gaming are **residual matters** falling
within the exclusive legislative competence of the States, nullified the National Lottery
Act 2005, and stripped the NLRC of its national licensing authority; the President declined
assent to the Central Gaming Bill 2025 in reliance on that judgment, so there is now **no
federal lottery statute** for Atlas to be exempt from (high confidence). The operative law
is State law, and in Atlas's launch market that is the **Lagos State Lotteries and Gaming
Authority Law 2021**, which (a) defines "Gaming" as *any* scheme "for distributing prizes by
lot or chance" **with no payment/consideration element at all**, (b) expressly classes
"promotional competitions" as "Other lotteries", (c) by s.86(1) prohibits operating or
promoting any "plan, scheme, **competition**, prize ... whether **remotely** or otherwise in
the State" without a licence, and (d) by its Remote Gaming Regulations 2021 requires any
remote provider serving a Lagos "State customer" to register and pay the gaming fee **even
where the player plays a free game with an opportunity to win prize money** (high confidence
— read from the gazetted text). Neither Nigerian statute examined contains **any** analogue
to UK Gambling Act 2005 s.14: the now-void federal definition went the other way and
expressly captured arrangements decided "as a result of the exercise of skill **and**
chance", and the Lagos definition needs only chance and a prize. **There is therefore no
skill carve-out and no free-entry carve-out to qualify for**, which makes the difficulty and
format of Atlas's skill question legally irrelevant in Nigeria (medium-high confidence —
based on reading the texts and finding no contrary Nigerian authority; the absence of
Nigerian case law directly on point is itself a finding). Of the three models, C is
marginally the most defensible and A the least, but the spread between them is small and all
three sit on the wrong side of the line: the decisive variable is **licensing, not
mechanics**. Before the regulator is even reached, two commercial gatekeepers bind: Paystack
prohibits "any other activity with an entry fee and a prize ... **games of skill (whether or
not it is legally defined as a lottery)** and sweepstakes" absent prior approval, and Google
Play's policy gives "games that accept money in exchange for an opportunity to win a
physical or monetary prize" as an **example of a violation** unless the developer holds a
gambling licence and completes Google's application. **Recommendation: engage Nigerian
gaming counsel now, and reframe the brief from "is our carve-out valid?" to "which State
licence do we need, at what cost, and is the business viable at that cost?"**

## 1. The statute

### 1.1 The federal position: National Lottery Act 2005 — definition, and its nullification

**The definition (s.57, interpretation).** Two independent transcriptions of the Act agree
on the operative words:

> "**'Lottery' or 'lotteries'** includes any game, scheme, arrangement, system, plan,
> **promotional competition** or device for the distribution of prizes by lot or chance, **or
> as a result of the exercise of skill and chance** or based on the outcome of sporting
> events, or any other game, scheme, arrangement, system, plan, competition or device, which
> the President may by notice in the Gazette declare to be lottery and which shall be
> operated according to a licence."
> — NLA 2005 s.57 ([PLAC transcription](https://www.placng.org/lawsofnigeria/print.php?sn=401);
> [GitHub transcription](https://github.com/mykeels/nigerian-laws/blob/master/2005/national-lottery-act-2005-no-7-2005.md);
> [LawCare Nigeria](https://lawcarenigeria.com/national-lottery-act/))

**Elements, read from the text (confidence: high on the text, medium on construction — no
Nigerian case applying it to a prize competition was found):**

- **Distribution of prizes** — required.
- **By lot or chance** — required, *or* by "skill and chance".
- **Payment / consideration** — **does not appear in the definition at all.** The Act's
  licensing hook (s.17) is the *operation of the business of a lottery*, not the taking of a
  payment.
- **"Promotional competition"** is named *inside* the definition of lottery, i.e. the drafter
  pulled promotional competitions **in**, not out.
- **Skill is expressly covered** where mixed with chance.

**Licensing requirement (s.17):**

> "As from the commencement of this Act, the operation of the business of a national lottery
> **or any lottery, by whatever name called**, shall be subject to a licence granted by the
> President upon recommendation by the Commission..."

The phrase "by whatever name called" is a direct answer to a renaming strategy. Atlas's copy
ban on the words "lottery" and "raffle" (PRD §1) is good consumer positioning and
*worthless* as a classification argument under this drafting.

**No exemptions.** Nothing in the reviewed text creates a private-lottery, small-lottery,
free-entry or skill exemption. (Medium-high confidence — based on fetched transcriptions and
targeted search; I did not obtain a certified Federal Government Printer copy.)

**But the Act is void.** On 22 November 2024 a seven-member panel of the Supreme Court in
suit **SC/1/2008**, *AG Lagos State & Ors v AG Federation*, held that the National Assembly
lacked competence to legislate on lottery for the States; lottery is not "trade and commerce"
on the Exclusive Legislative List and is a residual matter for State Houses of Assembly. The
Court held the Act (ss. 17–21 specifically, and the Act generally as applied to the States)
inconsistent with the 1999 Constitution.
([Channels TV](https://www.channelstv.com/2024/11/22/supreme-court-nullifies-national-lottery-act/);
[Templars client note, Jan 2025](https://www.templars-law.com/app/uploads/2025/01/Nullification-of-the-National-Lottery-Act-.pdf);
[ǼLEX](https://aelex.com/regulation-of-lotteries-in-nigeria-review-of-the-supreme-courts-decision-and-its-implications/);
[IMGL Magazine, Akpuchukwu & George, Mar 2025](https://www.imgl.org/publications/imgl-magazine-volume-3-no-1/legislative-and-regulatory-powers-over-lottery-gaming-and-betting-in-nigeria/))
(**High confidence** — multiple independent law-firm sources plus national press.)

Note the Court's own characterisation of lottery, as reported: participants "must give
something of value and a winner is selected by chance or luck or random drawing, **not by
skill**", and lottery is "inherently speculative and risk-based". That dictum is the single
strongest thread in Nigerian material for a skill argument — but it was reasoning about
*federal legislative competence*, not about whether a given product needs a licence, and the
statutory definitions that actually bind Atlas say the opposite. **Do not build a product on
it.** (Medium confidence on the quote's accuracy — taken from secondary reporting of the
judgment; I did not obtain the judgment text.)

**Central Gaming Bill 2025 — dead.** Passed by the National Assembly on 2–3 December 2025 to
re-federalise online gaming; President Tinubu declined assent, stating gaming is a residual
State matter.
([Guardian NG](https://guardian.ng/news/tinubu-declines-assent-to-central-gaming-bill/);
[Arise TV](https://www.arise.tv/tinubu-vows-not-to-sign-central-gaming-bill-says-it-is-states-matter/))
(High confidence.)

### 1.2 The position that actually governs Atlas: Lagos State Lotteries and Gaming Authority Law 2021

I obtained and read the gazetted Law (Law No. 7 of 2021, 67 pages, including the Casino
Gaming, Online and Retail Gaming, and Remote Gaming Regulations 2021) via
[srjlegal.com's copy](https://srjlegal.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/LAGOS-STATE-LOTTERIES-AND-GAMING-AUTHORITY-law-2021-1.pdf).
Quotes below are from that text. (**High confidence** on the text; medium on whether it has
since been amended — a 2024/2025/2026 amendment would not necessarily be online.)

**Definition — note the missing payment element:**

> "**'Gaming'** includes any game, scheme, arrangement, system, plan, or device for
> distributing prizes by lot or chance and any game, scheme, arrangement, system, plan or
> device, which the Authority may declare to be a game of chance"

> "**'Other lotteries'** means private lotteries, charitable lotteries, society lotteries and
> **promotional competitions** or any other lotteries as may be listed by the Authority"

> "**'Prize'** means the prize awarded to the winner of a game of chance and includes both a
> prize provided by a person organising gaming and winnings of money staked"

> "**'Remote gaming'** means any form of gaming which allows punters access to participate or
> game without being physically present in a certain location by using remote or online
> access..."

**The prohibition — s.86(1):**

> "A person shall not operate, promote, manage, distribute a plan, scheme, **competition**,
> **prize** or dividend, arrangement, system, game device, or equipment which directly or
> indirectly provides for betting or accepts wagers, gaming or playing any game of chance,
> issue a dividend or prize **whether remotely or otherwise in the State** except where—
> (a) it is conducting the appropriate licenced activity under this Law; (b) it is authorised
> for non-commercial gaming activity under this Law; or (c) it falls under gaming for private
> purposes."

**And s.33(3):**

> "A person without a subsisting licence or authorisation from the Authority shall not
> operate, conduct or deploy any equipment, manage or accept wagers, rent a property, employ
> any person or **make advertisements** for any gaming activity in the State."

**The free-entry point, head-on — Remote Gaming Regulations 2021, reg 6(5):**

> "Where a remote gaming provider allows a State customer to **play a free game with an
> opportunity to win prize money**, the gaming provider shall pay the appropriate gaming fee
> as stated in subregulation (1) to the Authority."

That is the Lagos drafter directly contemplating free play for prizes — and taxing it rather
than exempting it. It is the clearest single piece of evidence against Model B.

**Who is caught (Remote Gaming Regs, regs 2–5):** a "remote gaming provider" is a business
operating remote gaming that contracts directly with "State customers"; a State customer is
a person resident in Lagos, a body corporate with a Lagos office, or anyone the Authority
reasonably designates — and where a player gives no address, **they are registered as a Lagos
State customer by default**. Registration is required **at least 31 days before** allowing
State customers to participate, with a licence then issued. Failure to register is an
offence: **minimum fine ₦10,000,000 and/or up to 3 years' custodial sentence.**

**Licensing preconditions (s.35(1)):** CAC-registered company; minimum share capital
₦20,000,000 "or as may be directed by the Authority"; a local-content shareholder holding at
least **15%** *or* a **₦50,000,000 bank guarantee / surety bond**; demonstrated competence,
financial resources, data-protection measures, underage/vulnerable-person protections,
responsible-gaming rehabilitation measures, customer-protection and dispute mechanisms.
Under s.35(2), an application for an "other lotteries" licence must be accompanied by the
rules of the competition, the prize schedule per category of winner, and the intervals at
which it will run — and rules may only be altered with the Authority's prior approval.
Licences run 1 year, except a *lottery* licence which runs 10 years (s.41(1)).

**Sting in the tail for Atlas's marketing:** the Law's advertising code prohibits advertising
that "suggests that **skill can influence games that are purely games of chance**". Atlas's
core marketing device — foregrounding a skill question on a chance draw — would, once Atlas
is licensed, be the thing the advertising code bites on. (High confidence on the text;
untested in practice.)

## 2. Is there a Nigerian analogue to UK Gambling Act 2005 s.14 or the UK free-draw treatment?

**No. The space is absent, and where Nigerian drafters addressed the point they went the
opposite way.** (Medium-high confidence.)

| | UK (Gambling Act 2005) | Nigeria |
|---|---|---|
| Skill carve-out | s.14(5): arrangement is **not** a lottery if the skill/knowledge/judgement requirement would reasonably be expected to deter a significant proportion from entering, or prevent a significant proportion of entrants from winning | **None found.** NLA 2005 s.57 *expressly included* arrangements decided "as a result of the exercise of skill and chance". Lagos Law 2021 defines Gaming by chance + prize and defines "Other lotteries" to include promotional competitions. |
| Free-draw carve-out | A "free draw" with a genuine free entry route of equal prominence and odds is outside the lottery definition because there is no "payment to enter" | **None found.** Neither definition has a payment/consideration element to negate, and the Lagos Remote Gaming Regs expressly tax free-to-enter prize play (reg 6(5)). |
| Regulator guidance on the boundary | Gambling Commission publishes guidance on prize competitions and free draws | No equivalent Nigerian guidance located. |

**What fills the space?** Two things, neither of them a carve-out:

1. **The State licensing regime itself**, which has a *category* for what Atlas does
   ("Other lotteries" / promotional competitions) rather than an *exemption* for it. The
   FSGRN Universal Reciprocity Certificate explicitly covers "Online Sports Betting, Online
   Casino operations, Public Online Lottery services, and **Promotional Competitions**"
   ([Adeola Oyinlade & Co via Legal 500, 26 Jun 2026](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/nigeria/media-telecoms-it-entertainment/navigating-nigerias-new-gaming-laws-a-2026-regulatory-guide-for-sports-betting-and-casino-operators);
   [SiGMA on the 7 May 2025 signing](https://sigma.world/news/fsgrn-member-states-sign-subnational-reciprocity-licensing-framework/)).
   That is the opposite of an exemption: it is a named licence class.
2. **The FCCPC sales-promotion regime** (see §6), which governs promotions that *promote a
   primary product*. Atlas has no primary product — entries *are* the product — so this is
   probably the wrong door, and in any event it is a registration regime, not an exemption.

**One contrary commentary, flagged.** A LinkedIn article, "Sales Promotion Lottery and
Lottery Regulation in Nigeria" by Stanley Chukwuma, is quoted in search results as saying
that under the NLA "free entry draws are not lottery and are exempt from statutory control"
and that "if the marketer makes available alternative free entry routes in addition to one
payment route, the arrangement will not be considered as requiring payment." **I could not
access the article (HTTP 403) and have only the search-engine snippet.** It is a
practitioner's opinion piece, not statute, case law or regulator guidance; it reads the
payment element into a definition that does not contain one; and it predates the Supreme
Court judgment and the current State-led regime. Treat as **unverified and low weight** — but
do flag it to counsel, because if any Nigerian practitioner view supports Model B, this is
the thread to pull.
([URL, inaccessible](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sales-promotion-lottery-regulation-nigeria-stanley-chukwuma))

## 2A. Skill-question difficulty standard and answer format — the founder's direct question

### 2A.1 The logical prior, stated plainly

**There is no skill carve-out in Nigerian law that I could find, so there is no standard for
the skill question to meet, and no format rule to satisfy — because there is nothing to
qualify for.** Difficulty and format are, on the law as found, **moot in Nigeria**.
(Medium-high confidence: based on the text of NLA 2005 s.57, which captures skill-and-chance
arrangements; the Lagos Law 2021 definitions, which require only chance and a prize; the
Lagos advertising code, which treats "skill influences a game of chance" as a *misleading
claim*; and an absence of any Nigerian guidance to the contrary after targeted searching.
Confidence is not "high" because an absence of findings is weaker evidence than a positive
finding, and State-level guidelines issued by LSLGA or FSGRN may exist that are not online.)

Three corollaries the founder should internalise:

- **The skill question does not buy Atlas a legal position in Nigeria.** It may still have
  product value (friction, fraud reduction, positioning), but it is not a licence substitute.
- **Making the question harder does not improve Atlas's Nigerian legal position**, because
  the improvement would be measured against a test that does not exist in Nigerian law.
- **Conversely, the skill question creates a small new Nigerian risk**: under the Lagos
  advertising code, marketing that "suggests that skill can influence games that are purely
  games of chance" is prohibited.

### 2A.2 Nigerian difficulty standard — searched for, not found

- No provision in NLA 2005 or the Lagos Law 2021 addresses the difficulty of a skill element.
- No NLRC circular or State guideline addressing skill-question difficulty was located.
- No Nigerian case law on the point was located.
- The nearest Nigerian material is the Supreme Court dictum that a lottery winner is selected
  "by chance or luck or random drawing, **not by skill**" — which implies skill matters to
  characterisation but gives no threshold, and was said in a federalism context.
  **Status: unverified / no standard found.**

### 2A.3 Multiple choice — Nigerian position and UK comparison

**Nigeria:** nothing found addressing answer format. **Unverified.**

**UK, flagged expressly as an analogy and NOT as Nigerian law:** s.14(5) of the Gambling Act
2005 asks whether the skill, knowledge or judgement requirement "would reasonably be expected
to" (a) prevent a significant proportion of persons who wish to participate from doing so, or
(b) prevent a significant proportion of entrants from receiving a prize. Neither the Act nor
its explanatory notes defines "significant proportion", and the Gambling Commission has been
criticised for declining to quantify it. On format, the Commission's published position is
that **multiple-choice questions, and questions allowing a second attempt after a wrong
answer, rarely satisfy the test**, and that general-knowledge questions "where the answer can
be found with little or no effort, or in the accompanying text or narrative" are unlikely to
demonstrate sufficient skill.
([Gambling Commission, *Prize competitions and free draws: the requirements of the Gambling
Act 2005*](https://assets.ctfassets.net/j16ev64qyf6l/3pj85vOPWgkchLNLVUs9PV/92c9622bea378560e4ecb375e3f94364/Prize-competitions-and-free-draws-the-requirements-of-the-gambling-act-2005.pdf);
[Osborne Clarke marketinglaw](https://marketinglaw.osborneclarke.com/marketing-techniques/gambling-regulators-views-on-key-prize-promo-issues/);
[JMW](https://www.jmw.co.uk/blog/commercial-litigation-dispute-resolution/prize-competitions-and-the-gambling-act))
Multiple choice is nonetheless near-universal in the UK competition market, which is a
statement about enforcement appetite, not about the legal test.
(Medium-high confidence on the Commission's stated position; medium on the precise wording,
since the fetched PDF rendered imperfectly.)

### 2A.4 Assessment of Atlas's actual implementation

Against the UK s.14(5) test **as the nearest available benchmark only** (there is no Nigerian
benchmark), Atlas's current implementation would **probably fail**:

| Atlas implementation | Assessment against UK s.14(5) |
|---|---|
| Four-option multiple choice, ~25% success by pure guessing | Fails on its own: a 1-in-4 blind success rate does not "prevent a significant proportion of entrants from receiving a prize", and the Commission says MC rarely qualifies. |
| Small pool of active questions | Compounds the above — a motivated entrant learns the whole pool. |
| `HMAC(pepper, user‖draw‖minute) mod pool_size` → same user + same draw + same minute gets the **same** question, and each request mints a fresh attempt | This is functionally **unlimited retries within the minute**. The Commission singles out "questions that allow a second attempt if the first answer is wrong" as rarely meeting the standard. This is the most damaging single feature. |
| Planned bundles: one correct answer buys up to 10 entries | Severs skill from entry for 9 of 10 entries. Under a payment-and-chance analysis those 9 entries are allocated by chance alone. **This feature makes the position materially worse and should not ship without counsel sign-off.** |

**What would have to change to have a credible skill argument** (in a jurisdiction that has
one — Nigeria, on current findings, does not):
- Free-text or constructed answers, not 4-option MC; or MC with genuinely plausible
  distractors and a much larger, rotating pool.
- **One attempt per entry**, enforced server-side: bind the attempt to the entry, not to the
  request, and do not re-serve the same question to the same user after a wrong answer.
- **One skill answer per ticket**, including inside bundles — kill the 1-answer-buys-10
  design or require an answer per entry.
- Evidence collection: log per-question pass rates so Atlas can *show* a regulator that a
  significant proportion of entrants get it wrong. Atlas currently has no such telemetry
  requirement.

**Blunt framing for the founder:** today the skill question is a UX gate with legal
decoration. In Nigeria it buys nothing. If Atlas later expands to a jurisdiction with a real
skill carve-out, the current implementation would not qualify there either — so the
engineering changes above are worth making on their own merits, *after* the Nigerian
licensing question is settled, not instead of settling it.

## 3. NLRC interpretive posture — and who now holds the pen

**The NLRC is substantially defunct.** The Supreme Court judgment nullified the Act that
created it; law-firm commentary states that existing NLRC licences lost their legal
foundation. The NLRC survives, at most, with respect to Abuja, and even there day-to-day
regulation moved to the newly created **FCT Lottery Regulatory Office (FCT-LRO)** from May
2025. On **10 November 2026** the FCT-LRO gave former NLRC licensees seven days to begin
regularising into FCT-LRO licences, with unexpired NLRC licences recognised for their
remaining validity and converted.
([Templars](https://www.templars-law.com/app/uploads/2025/01/Nullification-of-the-National-Lottery-Act-.pdf);
[FocusGN on the FCT-LRO 7-day notice](https://focusgn.com/africa/nigeria-abuja-gaming-licences);
[PM News, 9 Nov 2026](https://pmnewsnigeria.com/2026/11/09/fct-unveils-own-gaming-regulator-gives-operators-7-day-ultimatum/);
[Lawyard](https://www.lawyard.org/news/fct-establishes-gaming-regulator-mandates-operator-compliance-amidst-evolving-legal-landscape/))
(Medium-high confidence; the NLRC's precise residual status is contested commentary rather
than settled law.)

**On promotional competitions specifically.** The only NLRC position I found is reported
second-hand: that a "promotional competition with a lottery element is governed by the
provisions of the National Lottery Act and regulations promulgated thereunder". The same
secondary source asserts that promotions not charging an entry payment were not treated as
lottery. **I could not access the underlying article and found no NLRC circular, guideline,
or published determination to confirm either proposition. Unverified; low confidence; and in
any event now of historical interest only, given the Act's nullification.**

**Enforcement posture of the regulators that *do* now matter is aggressive, not permissive:**

- LSLGA identified **59 illegal and unlicensed operators** in April 2026.
- By public notice dated **12 October 2026**, LSLGA gave MNOs, telcos and VAS providers **30
  days** to verify the licensing and tax status of every gaming service on their platforms,
  demanded transaction, carrier-billing and revenue-share records back to **22 November
  2024**, ordered them to stop onboarding unlicensed gaming operators, and stated that an NCC
  telecoms/VAS licence "does not constitute a licence or authorization to conduct or
  facilitate lottery, betting or gaming activities in Lagos State". The window closed
  mid-November 2026, after which LSLGA said it "will then issue statutory notices and pursue
  all available enforcement measures."
  ([Launch Base Africa, 14 Oct 2026](https://launchbaseafrica.com/2026/10/14/the-%E2%82%A6100m-question-facing-lagos-gaming-founders/);
  [Lawyard](https://www.lawyard.org/news/lagos-mandates-telecoms-to-police-gaming-licences-amidst-regulatory-scrutiny/))
  (Medium confidence — reported by niche/trade press; I did not obtain the LSLGA notice
  itself.)

**Has anyone tested the Atlas model in Nigeria?** **Not verified.** Search surfaced consumer
sites with names suggesting the model (e.g. "NaijaWinners — Free-to-Play Prize Promotion
Platform", "Raffle Naija"); `naijawinners.com` returns **HTTP 404** and I could not
substantiate any of them as an operating Nigerian business, let alone one that has been
tested by a regulator. **I found no Nigerian operator of a BOTB/Omaze-style paid prize
competition, and no enforcement action for or against such a model.** This is a genuine gap
and a high-value question for counsel, who will have market knowledge that is not online.

## 4. Federal vs State — which applies to an online operator

**Resolved, and not the way the counsel brief assumes.** (High confidence.)

- **Federal:** there is no operative federal lottery statute. NLA 2005 is void as to the
  States; the Central Gaming Bill 2025 was refused assent. The federal footprint is limited
  to the **FCT**, now via the FCT-LRO.
- **State:** lottery and gaming are residual matters; each State House of Assembly legislates
  for its own territory. Lagos's regime is the LSLGA Law 2021 plus its 2021 Regulations.
- **Online/remote operators:** Lagos solves the territoriality problem by regulating the
  *customer*, not the server. A "State customer" includes anyone resident in Lagos — and
  anyone who supplies no address at all. An operator serving them is a "remote gaming
  provider" that must register 31 days ahead and pay the gaming fee regardless of where it
  is located ("regardless of the location of the remote gaming provider", reg 6(2)). **An
  online Nigerian consumer product cannot avoid Lagos.**
- **How multi-State is resolved in practice:** the **Federation of State Gaming Regulators of
  Nigeria (FSGRN)** adopted a Subnational Reciprocity Licensing Framework on **7 May 2025**,
  creating a **Universal Reciprocity Certificate (URC)** — a single State licence (most
  commonly LSLGA's) recognised by other member States. Reported coverage is ~20–25 of 36
  States as of mid-2026, and the URC **expressly covers Promotional Competitions**. States
  outside FSGRN are not covered, and ǼLEX flags as unresolved whether an operator based in
  one State must separately license in every State where it has remote customers.
  ([SiGMA](https://sigma.world/news/fsgrn-member-states-sign-subnational-reciprocity-licensing-framework/);
  [Legal 500 / Adeola Oyinlade & Co](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/nigeria/media-telecoms-it-entertainment/navigating-nigerias-new-gaming-laws-a-2026-regulatory-guide-for-sports-betting-and-casino-operators);
  [ǼLEX](https://aelex.com/regulation-of-lotteries-in-nigeria-review-of-the-supreme-courts-decision-and-its-implications/))
  (Medium-high confidence on the URC's existence and scope; medium on State counts.)

**Practical answer: Lagos first, via LSLGA, then ride the URC for national reach, then
State-by-State audit for non-FSGRN States.**

## 5. Consequences of misclassification

### 5.1 Penalties — criminal, not merely civil

**Lagos State Lotteries and Gaming Authority Law 2021** (quoted from the gazetted text;
**high confidence**):

| Conduct | Penalty |
|---|---|
| Operating/promoting an unlicensed plan, scheme, **competition** or prize, remotely or otherwise (s.86(1)–(2)) | Custodial sentence up to **3 years**, or **minimum fine ₦5,000,000**, or both |
| Conducting, facilitating, promoting **or deriving any benefit from** unauthorised gaming (s.86(3)) | Max fine **₦5,000,000** and/or up to **3 years** |
| Remote gaming provider failing to register 31 days before serving State customers (Remote Gaming Regs reg 5(3)) | **Minimum fine ₦10,000,000**, and/or custodial sentence up to **3 years** |
| Late/non-payment of the gaming fee (reg 6(6)) | **10% of the outstanding fee per day of default**, capped at twice the fee |
| Early termination of the bank guarantee/surety arrangement without consent (s.39(5)) | Minimum fine **₦10,000,000** |

Note s.86(3) reaches *facilitators and beneficiaries*, not just the operator — relevant to
directors, agents and arguably marketing partners. Note also that s.33(3) makes **advertising**
an unlicensed gaming activity an offence in its own right: Atlas could incur liability from
a pre-launch marketing campaign alone.

**NLA 2005 (now void, recorded for completeness):** s.34 declared unlicensed national/
inter-State lottery operation unlawful, with fines from ₦20,000 and imprisonment from one
year for individuals, and ₦100,000+ for bodies corporate with matching director liability.
These figures are two decades stale and are no longer the live exposure.

## 6. Adjacent regulators

### 6.1 FCCPC — sales promotions

The Consumer Protection (Sales Promotions) Regulations 2005 require **registration of sales
promotions** with the FCCPC, payment of prescribed fees, and issuance of a registration
certificate. **Regulation 3 provides that promotions constituting lotteries under the NLA
2005 must comply with that Act and seek NLRC approval** — a cross-reference now broken by the
nullification, and a live question for counsel as to what replaced it.

The FCCPC published **Draft Sales Promotion (Amended) Regulations, 2026**, out for public
consultation, which revise notification fees, extend expressly to **online promotions,
influencer marketing, AI-generated campaigns and foreign businesses targeting Nigerian
consumers**, and exempt promotions with total prize value **≤ ₦500,000** from fees via a
simplified notification (without relief from fairness/transparency duties).
([FCCPC](https://fccpc.gov.ng/businesses/register-sales-promotion/);
[Detail Solicitors, 13 Nov 2026](https://www.detailsolicitors.com/analysis-of-nigerias-draft-sales-promotion-regulations-2026/);
[Financial Nigeria](https://www.financialnigeria.com/analysis-of-nigeria-s-draft-sales-promotion-regulations-2026-feature-667.html);
[Concurrences](https://www.concurrences.com/en/bulletin/news-issues/preview/the-nigerian-competition-authority-launches-a-public-consultation-on-draft))
(Medium confidence; the draft text itself was not obtained.)

**My analysis, flagged as analysis:** a "sales promotion" presupposes a primary product or
service being promoted. Atlas has none — the entry *is* the product. So Atlas is more likely
to be a gaming operator than a promoter, and the FCCPC route is probably **not** an
alternative door to licensing. **But this is my reading, not a sourced Nigerian position —
put it to counsel.** Generic FCCPA consumer-protection duties (no misleading representations
about odds or prize values) apply regardless of classification.

### 6.2 APCON → ARCON — advertising

- **APCON no longer exists in the form the counsel brief assumes.** The Advertising
  Regulatory Council of Nigeria (**ARCON**) replaced it in 2022 under the Advertising
  Regulation Act.
- ARCON operates a **pre-exposure vetting/approval** regime for advertisements.
- **ARCON and FSGRN signed an MoU harmonising gaming advertising rules across 25 member
  States, with Harmonised Advertising Rules effective 1 April 2026**, mandating consistent
  standards and responsible-gambling messaging across all media.
  ([TheCable](https://www.thecable.ng/arcon-state-regulators-sign-mou-to-harmonise-advertising-rules-for-gaming-industry/);
  [BrandCrunch](https://brandcrunch.com.ng/2026/03/09/arcon-25-states-align-to-clean-up-gaming-ads-strengthen-consumer-protection/);
  [FocusGN](https://focusgn.com/africa/arcon-and-state-gaming-regulators-sign-mou-to-unify-gambling-advertising-rules-in-nigeria))
- Some States (Ondo, Enugu, Oyo) have asserted their own pre-exposure vetting of gaming ads;
  ARCON has publicly objected. Expect friction.
  ([TheCable](https://www.thecable.ng/arcon-condemns-ondo-enugu-directives-requiring-review-of-political-gaming-ads-before-exposure/);
  [Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2026/01/oyo-agencies-defend-vetting-of-betting-adverts-say-move-complements-arcons-role/))
- Plus, under the Lagos Law directly: **no advertising of any gaming activity without a
  subsisting LSLGA licence** (s.33(3)), and no advertising that "suggests that skill can
  influence games that are purely games of chance".

(Medium-high confidence on ARCON's existence/role; medium on the MoU's operative detail.)

## 7. Commercial gatekeepers — these bind before the regulator does

### 7.1 Paystack

**Paystack's ineligible-business policy is broader than the law and catches Atlas expressly,
skill question and all** (high confidence — quoted from Paystack's own support pages):

> "gambling, gaming and/or any other activity **with an entry fee and a prize**, including,
> but not limited to, casino games, sports betting, horse or greyhound racing, lottery
> tickets, other ventures that facilitate gambling, **games of skill (whether or not it is
> legally defined as a lottery) and sweepstakes** unless the operator has obtained **prior
> approval from Paystack** and the operator and customers are located exclusively in
> jurisdictions where such activities are permitted by law."
> — [Paystack, "Ineligible businesses"](https://support.paystack.com/en/articles/2127042)

The same page separately lists "Lottery tickets, sweepstakes entries or slot machines
**without the required licence**".

Paystack **does** support Nigerian gaming merchants, but as **"Required Registered
Businesses"** — betting companies and lottery businesses must supply registration documents
to activate.
([Paystack, "Supported businesses"](https://support.paystack.com/en/articles/2129730);
[Paystack, high-risk merchants](https://support.paystack.com/en/articles/5949186))
Paystack's published pages do **not** enumerate which documents, and **do not state in terms
that an LSLGA/NLRC licence is required** — that is the obvious inference but it is
**unverified**. (Medium confidence overall; the quoted prohibition text is high confidence.)

**Consequence for Atlas:** the parenthetical "(whether or not it is legally defined as a
lottery)" is fatal to the strategy of arguing Atlas out of the gaming category at the payment
layer. Atlas cannot onboard quietly as an e-commerce merchant; it needs an affirmative
Paystack approval decision, which will in practice turn on licensing evidence. **PRD §3.4
assumes Paystack as the sole V1 rail. This is a single point of failure that should be tested
with Paystack's compliance team in Phase 0, before further build.** A misclassified
onboarding followed by a later freeze would strand customer funds mid-draw — which, for a
trust-first brand, is worse than not launching.

### 7.2 Apple — App Store Review Guideline 5.3

Quoted from the live guidelines (fetched 2026-11-23; **high confidence**):

> **5.3.1** Sweepstakes and contests must be sponsored by the developer of the app.
> **5.3.2** Official rules for sweepstakes, contests, and raffles must be presented in the
> app and make clear that Apple is not a sponsor or involved in the activity in any manner.
> **5.3.3** Apps may not use in-app purchase to purchase credit or currency for use in
> conjunction with real money gaming of any kind.
> **5.3.4** Apps that offer real money gaming (such as sports betting, poker, casino games,
> horse racing) or lotteries must have **necessary licensing and permissions in the locations
> where the app is used**, must be **geo-restricted to those locations**, and must be **free
> on the App Store**. ... **Lottery apps must have consideration, chance, and a prize.**
> — [Apple, App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)

**Reading for Atlas:** 5.3.4's own test — *consideration, chance, prize* — is met by paid
entries into a chance draw. The free-entry route does not remove consideration from the paid
path. So Atlas should expect App Review to apply 5.3.4: **licensing evidence for Nigeria,
geo-fencing to Nigeria, free download, 18+.** 5.3.1–5.3.2 are easy (Atlas sponsors its own
draws; publish full rules in-app). 5.3.3 is manageable only because Atlas charges via
Paystack rather than IAP — but note the tension: Apple requires digital goods to use IAP
while forbidding IAP for real-money-gaming credit, and the way that is reconciled in practice
is by the app being classified as real-money gaming, which pulls 5.3.4 back in.
(High confidence on the text; **medium on how App Review would classify Atlas specifically —
that is a judgement call made by a reviewer, and the only way to de-risk it is a pre-submission
conversation and a licence in hand.**)

### 7.3 Google Play — Real-Money Gambling, Games, and Contests

Quoted from the live policy (fetched 2026-11-23; **high confidence**):

> "we allow apps that enable or facilitate online gambling in **select countries**, as long as
> the Developer completes the **application process**..., is an approved governmental operator
> and/or is **registered as a licensed operator with the appropriate governmental gambling
> authority** in the specified country, and provides a **valid operating license** ..."
>
> Eligible apps must: complete Google's application; comply with local law; hold "a valid
> gambling license for **each country or state/territory** in which the app is distributed";
> not exceed the scope of that licence; **prevent under-age users**; **prevent access from
> areas not covered by the licence**; **not be a paid app and not use Google Play In-app
> Billing**; be **free to download**; be rated **AO (Adult Only)** or IARC equivalent; and
> display responsible-gambling information.
>
> For everything else: "we don't allow content or services that enable or facilitate users'
> ability to wager, stake, or participate using real money ... to obtain a prize of real world
> monetary value", with an express example of a violation being **"Games that accept money in
> exchange for an opportunity to win a physical or monetary prize."**
> — [Google Play Policy Center](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9877032)

The permitted product types are: Online Casino games, Sports Betting, Horse Racing, **Lotteries**,
Daily Fantasy Sports. **There is no "prize competition" or "skill competition" category.**
Atlas would have to enter as a **Lottery**, which requires a lottery licence.

**Is Nigeria on Google's permitted list?** Secondary sources say yes, with Africa coverage
spanning Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Tanzania and Ethiopia, and one source stating Nigeria is
permitted only for **selected States (Cross River, Ekiti, Enugu, Imo, Lagos, Oyo)** subject to
authorisation from the relevant State gambling authority — though that source appears to be
describing **Google Ads** policy, which is a different regime.
([Talk Android](https://www.talkandroid.com/501942-online-gambling-apps-where-the-play-store-does-and-doesnt-allow-them/);
[CrustLab](https://crustlab.com/blog/google-allows-gambling-apps-in-multiple-countries/);
[Google Ads gambling application](https://support.google.com/google-ads/troubleshooter/2893932))
**Google's own Play page does not publish the country list, and I could not verify Nigeria's
status from a Google source. Treat as medium-low confidence / partially unverified** — but
note that Nigerian press has historically covered the *absence* of betting apps from Play in
Nigeria ([Blueprint](https://blueprint.ng/why-does-the-google-playstore-not-have-betting-apps-in-nigeria/)),
which is a warning sign.

**Consequence for Atlas:** PRD V1 is **Flutter on iOS + Android**. On both stores the path is
the same — **hold a licence, geo-fence, go free-to-download, age-gate** — and on Google the
app must additionally carry an AO rating and complete Google's gambling application. **There
is no store route for an unlicensed paid prize-draw app.** This should be treated as a
distribution risk of the same severity as the regulatory risk, and it argues for adding a
mobile web/PWA fallback to the V1 architecture rather than depending solely on store
distribution.

## 8. Ranking the three models by defensibility under Nigerian law

**First, the honest framing: all three are on the wrong side of the line, and the ranking is
between degrees of exposure, not between "compliant" and "non-compliant".** The variable that
decides Atlas's legality in Nigeria is whether it holds a State gaming licence — not which
entry mechanic it ships.

### Model C — free entry route **and** skill question (the current build)
**Rank: 1st (least bad). Confidence: medium.**
Best of the three because it preserves every argument that exists: the free route supports an
(unsupported-by-statute) "no consideration" argument, and the skill element gives counsel
something to point at if a regulator is willing to read the Supreme Court's "not by skill"
dictum broadly. It is also the only model that would travel to a jurisdiction with a real
carve-out. **But** it does not escape the Lagos definitions, s.86(1) still bites on a
"competition" or "prize" scheme operated remotely, and the free route attracts the gaming fee
under Remote Gaming Reg 6(5) rather than an exemption. Confidence is only medium because the
ranking rests on "more arguments is better", which is a weak principle when none of the
arguments has Nigerian authority behind it.

### Model B — free entry route at equal odds, no skill question
**Rank: 2nd. Confidence: medium-low.**
The consideration argument is the only one with any theoretical traction, because it is the
one point where a Nigerian practitioner view (the inaccessible LinkedIn piece) appears to
exist. Against it: **neither** operative definition contains a payment element to negate — the
Lagos definition of "Gaming" is chance + prize, full stop — and the Lagos drafter expressly
addressed free-to-enter prize play and **taxed it**. Confidence is medium-low because the one
supporting source is unverified commentary and because the counter-text (Reg 6(5)) is direct
and unambiguous.

### Model A — skill question on every paid entry, no free route
**Rank: 3rd (weakest). Confidence: medium-high that this is the weakest of the three.**
Model A is the one the Nigerian drafting defeats *in terms*: the now-void federal definition
expressly captured arrangements decided "as a result of the exercise of **skill and chance**",
and the Lagos regime recognises "games of chance" with no skill exemption while its
advertising code treats "skill influences a chance game" as a misleading claim. Removing the
free route also removes the only consumer-protection feature that might soften a regulator's
view, and it makes the product indistinguishable from a paid lottery with a quiz attached.
Confidence here is higher than for the others because it rests on a positive statutory
statement rather than on an absence.

### What the ranking means in practice

1. **Keep Model C** — it costs little and preserves optionality.
2. **Do not treat the ranking as a launch decision.** The question "A, B or C?" is
   downstream of "licensed or not?", and the answer to that is **licence required**
   (medium-high confidence).
3. **Fix the skill implementation anyway** (§2A.4) — specifically kill the
   one-answer-buys-10-entries bundle design and the repeatable-attempt behaviour — so that
   the model is at least coherent with how Atlas describes itself.
4. **Rewrite PRD §1 and counsel brief §3 Q1** to reflect the post-2024 legal landscape before
   spending counsel hours.
